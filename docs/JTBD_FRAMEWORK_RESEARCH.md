## 1. Core Theory

### What is JTBD?

Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) is a theory of innovation that holds that **people don't buy products -- they hire them to get a job done**. A "job" is the task, goal, or objective a customer is trying to accomplish in a given circumstance. The theory shifts focus away from the product itself and toward the underlying progress a person is trying to make in their life.

The central insight: customers don't want a quarter-inch drill bit -- they want a quarter-inch hole. And they don't even want the hole; they want to hang a picture on the wall to make their home feel decorated and personal.

### The Three Founders

**Tony Ulwick** -- Conceptualized JTBD in 1990 by applying Six Sigma thinking to the innovation process. In 1999, he officially named his innovation methodology **Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI)** and introduced it to Clayton Christensen. Ulwick's approach is the most structured and quantitative of the three, treating jobs as primarily functional and measurable. His key works include *What Customers Want* (2005) and *Jobs to Be Done: Theory to Practice* (2016). He founded Strategyn, a consulting firm built entirely around ODI.

**Clayton Christensen** -- Harvard Business School professor who popularized JTBD through his 2003 book *The Innovator's Solution* and later devoted an entire book to the theory: *Competing Against Luck* (2016). Christensen positioned JTBD as a broader theory of innovation and causality, arguing that understanding the job is the key to predicting which innovations will succeed. His approach is more qualitative and narrative-driven than Ulwick's. Christensen passed away in January 2020.

**Bob Moesta** -- Co-developed the JTBD framework with Christensen through thousands of real-world customer interviews. Moesta's contribution is the most practitioner-oriented: he developed the **Switch Interview** methodology, the **Four Forces of Progress** diagram, and the six-stage buying timeline. His key works include *Demand-Side Sales 101* (2020) and *Learning to Build* (2022). He co-founded The Re-Wired Group.

### Relationship Between the Three

Christensen's work is often described as the **theory** -- a conceptual lens for understanding why customers make choices. Ulwick's ODI is a **quantitative methodology** -- step-by-step processes for measuring and prioritizing customer needs. Moesta's approach is a **qualitative methodology** -- interview techniques and frameworks for uncovering the causal story behind customer decisions.

There is some disagreement between Ulwick and Moesta/Christensen. Ulwick defines jobs as purely functional tasks (e.g., "listen to music"), treating emotional and social dimensions as separate need types. Moesta and Christensen define jobs as inherently incorporating functional, emotional, and social dimensions simultaneously.

### Key Principles

1. **Jobs are stable; products are not.** The job customers are trying to get done remains constant even as technologies, products, and competitors change. People have been "sharing moments with friends" for millennia -- the solutions have evolved from letters to phones to Instagram.

2. **A market is defined by the job, not the product or demographic.** A group of people + the core functional job they are trying to get done = a market. Segmenting by demographics or product category misses the underlying causality.

3. **Customers are not just choosing a product; they are making progress.** Every purchase is a story about someone trying to move from a current situation they find unsatisfying toward a better one.

4. **Circumstances matter enormously.** The same person in different circumstances has different jobs. A parent driving alone to work has a different milkshake job than the same parent buying a milkshake for their child on a Saturday afternoon.

5. **Innovation becomes predictable when the job is understood.** Christensen argued that understanding the job removes the "luck" from innovation. The title *Competing Against Luck* captures his thesis: you don't need luck if you deeply understand the job.

## 2. The Four Forces of Progress

Developed by Bob Moesta, the **Four Forces of Progress** diagram explains why customers switch (or fail to switch) from one solution to another. Two forces drive change; two forces resist it. A switch happens only when the driving forces overcome the restraining forces.

```
                DRIVING FORCES                    RESTRAINING FORCES
         (motivate switching)                 (prevent switching)

    +---------------------------+        +---------------------------+
    |  F1: Push of the          |        |  F3: Anxiety of the       |
    |      Current Situation    |        |      New Solution         |
    +---------------------------+        +---------------------------+
    |  F2: Pull of the          |        |  F4: Habit of the         |
    |      New Solution         |        |      Present              |
    +---------------------------+        +---------------------------+

         >>> SWITCH >>>              vs.         <<< STAY <<<
```

### Force 1: Push of the Current Situation

The **struggles, frustrations, and dissatisfactions** in the customer's current situation that push them to seek a better solution. Push is problem-awareness -- something in the status quo is no longer acceptable.

- A commuter frustrated by a boring 45-minute drive (milkshake story)
- A SaaS user whose tool crashes during important presentations
- A viewer who keeps getting recommended content that wastes their time

Push alone is rarely enough to cause a switch. People tolerate enormous amounts of pain before acting. Moesta notes that push usually requires a **triggering event** -- a moment that crystallizes the dissatisfaction into urgency. ("The software crashed during the board meeting, and I looked like an idiot. That was the last straw.")

### Force 2: Pull of the New Solution

The **attraction, magnetism, and promise** of a new solution that draws the customer toward it. Pull is aspiration -- the imagined better future the new solution represents.

- "This new tool has a beautiful interface and is so much faster"
- "I heard this podcast explains complex topics in a way I actually understand"
- "My friend told me this channel completely changed how she thinks about money"

Pull is typically what marketers focus on -- features, benefits, testimonials. But pull without push is weak. If there is no struggle pushing someone away from the status quo, even the most attractive new solution will not motivate a switch.

### Force 3: Anxiety of the New Solution

The **fears, concerns, and uncertainties** about the new solution that hold the customer back. Anxiety is risk-awareness -- "What if the new thing doesn't work? What if I can't figure it out? What if I lose my data?"

Common anxieties:
- **Performance anxiety**: "Will it actually work as promised?"
- **Learning anxiety**: "Will I have to learn a whole new system?"
- **Social anxiety**: "What will people think of me if I switch?"
- **Financial anxiety**: "What if I'm wasting my money?"
- **Identity anxiety**: "Is this really the kind of product someone like me uses?"

Anxiety is one of the most underestimated forces. Products can have massive push and strong pull, but if the anxiety is high, the customer freezes. This is why free trials, money-back guarantees, demos, and social proof exist -- they are all anxiety-reduction mechanisms.

### Force 4: Habit of the Present

The **inertia, familiarity, and comfort** of the current way of doing things. Even if the current solution is objectively worse, switching has costs: time, effort, re-learning, giving up familiar workflows.

- "I know it's not perfect, but I know exactly how it works"
- "All my files are in this format, switching would be a nightmare"
- "I've been watching this creator for three years -- I'm loyal"

Habit is the silent killer of innovation. Moesta emphasizes that **people will endure remarkable amounts of pain rather than face the discomfort of change**. Habit includes both behavioral inertia (routines) and emotional attachment (loyalty, identity, sunk cost).

### The Balance

A switch occurs when: **Push + Pull > Anxiety + Habit**

The practical implication: innovators must either increase push/pull or decrease anxiety/habit. The best products do all four:
- They make the current pain vivid (push)
- They present an attractive, clear alternative (pull)
- They reduce risk through trials, demonstrations, guarantees (reduce anxiety)
- They make switching easy through migration tools, onboarding, compatibility (reduce habit friction)

## 3. Functional, Emotional, and Social Jobs

Every job has three dimensions. Understanding all three is critical for differentiation, because competitors who only address the functional job leave the emotional and social dimensions as exploitable gaps.

### Functional Jobs

The **practical, tangible task** the customer is trying to accomplish. Functional jobs are typically the most visible and measurable.

- "Get from point A to point B" (transportation)
- "Understand my monthly spending" (personal finance)
- "Learn how macroeconomics works" (education)
- "Stay informed about developments in my industry" (news/media)

Functional jobs are where Ulwick focuses most of his ODI methodology. They can be broken down into process steps (a "job map"), and each step has measurable desired outcomes.

### Emotional Jobs

How the customer **wants to feel** (or wants to avoid feeling) as a result of doing the functional job. Emotional jobs are personal and internal.

- **Desired feelings**: confident, secure, accomplished, entertained, relaxed, inspired, smart
- **Avoided feelings**: stupid, anxious, overwhelmed, bored, guilty, embarrassed, confused

Examples:
- All cars address the functional job of transportation, but a Tesla gives the driver a feeling of environmental responsibility, a Porsche gives the joy of performance driving, and a Rolls-Royce gives a sense of accomplishment and success.
- Two financial apps both track spending, but one makes the user feel in control and empowered while the other makes them feel judged and guilty.

### Social Jobs

How the customer **wants to be perceived by others** as a result of using the product. Social jobs are external -- about identity, status, and group belonging.

- "I want to be seen as someone who is well-informed" (hiring The Economist)
- "I want to be perceived as a good parent" (hiring organic baby food)
- "I want my colleagues to see me as innovative" (hiring a cutting-edge tool)
- "I want to be part of a community that values learning" (hiring a specific YouTube channel)

### How They Interact

The three dimensions always coexist, but their relative importance varies by context.

**Functional-dominant jobs**: Utilitarian purchases where the task matters most. Example: buying a USB cable. The emotional and social dimensions exist but are minimal.

**Emotional-dominant jobs**: Purchases where feelings drive the decision. Example: choosing a therapist, picking a comfort food, selecting background music. The functional job is simple; the emotional job is what differentiates.

**Social-dominant jobs**: Purchases where perception by others is the primary driver. Example: luxury goods, social media platform choice, certain education credentials. The product is a signal.

**The critical insight for differentiation**: In crowded markets where functional jobs are well-served by multiple competitors, emotional and social jobs become the primary battleground. Content creators, in particular, compete heavily on emotional and social dimensions because the functional job ("learn about X topic") is often served by dozens of channels.

## 4. Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI)

Tony Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation is the most structured, quantitative approach to JTBD. It provides a systematic method for identifying which customer needs represent the best innovation opportunities.

### Core Process

1. **Define the core functional job** the customer is trying to get done
2. **Create a job map** -- break the job into its constituent process steps (typically 5-8 steps)
3. **Capture desired outcome statements** for each step
4. **Survey customers** on importance and satisfaction for each outcome
5. **Apply the Opportunity Algorithm** to identify underserved and overserved needs
6. **Segment the market** based on outcome patterns
7. **Formulate a winning strategy** targeting the identified opportunities

### Desired Outcome Statements

Ulwick invented a specific format for expressing customer needs, called **desired outcome statements**. Each statement has four components:

**Direction of improvement + Unit of measure + Object of control + Contextual clarifier**

Examples:
- "Minimize the time it takes to get the songs in the desired order for listening"
- "Minimize the likelihood of losing important data during a system update"
- "Minimize the time it takes to identify which expenses are tax-deductible"

Key characteristics of well-formed outcome statements:
- **Devoid of solutions** (never reference a product or technology)
- **Stable over time** (the outcome remains relevant regardless of how technology changes)
- **Measurable** (can be quantified)
- **Controllable** (the innovator can influence this outcome)
- **Structured for reliable survey prioritization**

For any given core functional job, there are typically **50 to 150 desired outcomes** that customers use to measure success, plus 10-30 outcomes per consumption chain job (acquiring, setting up, learning, maintaining, etc.).

### The Opportunity Algorithm

The formula that identifies where innovation opportunities exist:

```
Opportunity Score = Importance + max(Importance - Satisfaction, 0)
```

This gives **twice as much weight to importance** compared to simple gap analysis. The score ranges from 0 to 20 (when both importance and satisfaction are measured on a 0-10 scale).

### Interpreting the Scores

| Score Range | Classification | Strategy |
|------------|---------------|----------|
| > 12 | **Underserved** | High importance, low satisfaction. Best opportunities for innovation. Customers are struggling here. |
| 8 - 12 | **Appropriately served** | Importance roughly matches satisfaction. Maintain current performance; incremental improvements. |
| < 8 | **Overserved** | Low importance, high satisfaction. Customers are over-satisfied. Resources can be redirected. Opportunity for disruptive (simpler, cheaper) solutions. |

### Three Innovation Strategies

Based on ODI analysis, Ulwick identifies three strategies:

1. **Differentiated strategy**: Target underserved outcomes. Build a solution that satisfies important but poorly served needs better than any competitor. Premium positioning.

2. **Dominant strategy**: Address all underserved outcomes at once. Extremely difficult but creates category-defining products (e.g., the iPhone at launch).

3. **Disruptive strategy**: Target overserved outcomes. Strip away features that over-deliver on unimportant outcomes, offer a simpler/cheaper solution. Classic Christensen disruption.

### Survey Methodology

Ulwick's process surveys 120 to 1,200 customers. For each desired outcome statement, respondents rate:
- **Importance**: "How important is this outcome to you?" (1-5 or 1-10 scale)
- **Satisfaction**: "How satisfied are you with how well your current solution achieves this outcome?" (1-5 or 1-10 scale)

The percentage of respondents rating 4-5 (on a 5-point scale) or 8-10 (on a 10-point scale) is used as the importance and satisfaction values in the opportunity algorithm.

## 5. Switch Interviews / Timeline

The Switch Interview is the primary qualitative research method in the Moesta/Christensen school of JTBD. Developed by Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek, it reconstructs the **full story of a real switching decision** -- from the first thought that something needed to change through to life after adoption.

### The Six-Stage Buying Timeline

```
Timeline: ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────>

 (1)           (2)              (3)            (4)         (5)          (6)
First        Passive          Active        Deciding   Onboarding   Ongoing
Thought      Looking          Looking                                Use
  |             |                |              |           |          |
  v             v                v              v           v          v
"Something   "I casually     "I'm actively  "I'm making  "I'm        "This is
 isn't        notice          comparing      trade-offs   setting     now part
 right"       alternatives"   options"       and          it up"      of my
                                             choosing"                routine"
```

### Stage 1: First Thought

The moment the person first realized something needed to change. This is often tied to a specific event or trigger -- not a gradual awareness, but a crystallizing moment.

Key questions:
- "When did you first think about switching?"
- "What was happening in your life at that moment?"
- "Was there a specific event that triggered this?"

### Stage 2: Passive Looking

Casual, unfocused exploration. The person is not yet committed to switching but starts noticing alternatives. They might mention a product to a friend, see an ad, or stumble across a review.

Key questions:
- "What did you start noticing differently?"
- "Did you talk to anyone about this?"
- "How long were you in this phase?"

### Stage 3: Active Looking

Focused, deliberate research. The person has decided they want to change and is now comparing options, reading reviews, asking for recommendations, and evaluating trade-offs.

Key questions:
- "What specifically were you looking for?"
- "What options did you consider?"
- "What criteria were you using to compare?"

Moesta notes that moving from passive to active looking often requires a **"time wall"** -- an artificial deadline or event that creates urgency ("I need to have this figured out by the conference next month").

### Stage 4: Deciding

Making trade-offs and establishing value. The person narrows options and makes a final choice. Moesta observes that **people need something to reject before they can buy something else** -- the decision is as much about what they're saying "no" to as what they're saying "yes" to.

Key questions:
- "What made you choose this option over the others?"
- "What did you give up by making this choice?"
- "What was the final moment of deciding?"

### Stage 5: Onboarding

The implementation phase. First experiences with the new solution. This is where many switches fail -- the product might be good, but if onboarding is painful, the customer may revert.

Key questions:
- "What was your first experience like?"
- "Was there anything confusing or frustrating?"
- "Did the product match what you expected?"

### Stage 6: Ongoing Use

Building new habits and routines around the product. The new solution becomes the status quo -- which means it is now subject to the same forces that caused the previous switch.

Key questions:
- "How has this become part of your routine?"
- "Is there anything you miss about the old way?"
- "Would you recommend this to someone else?"

### Interview Methodology

- **Sample size**: 8 to 12 people who recently made a switching decision (ideally within the last 30-90 days, while memory is fresh)
- **Mix**: Include both "hirers" (chose your product) and "firers" (left your product) in roughly equal numbers
- **Approach**: Tell the participant you want to hear the story of how they went from first thought to where they are now. Use the timeline as a visual guide during the interview.
- **Technique**: Work backward from the purchase moment. "Tell me about the day you signed up. Now, what happened right before that? And before that?" This reverse chronology helps surface details that forward-narration skips.
- **Duration**: Typically 30-60 minutes per interview
- **What to listen for**: Energy levels, emotional language, specific details about events and context. Moesta says the goal is to find "the energy and how much energy they spent to make progress and how they overcame the anxiety and the habit of the present."

### Mapping the Four Forces onto the Timeline

During the interview, you map where each force appears:
- **Push** is strongest at First Thought and during the struggling moment
- **Pull** emerges during Passive and Active Looking
- **Anxiety** peaks during Deciding and Onboarding
- **Habit** is present throughout but most visible when the person describes resistance to change

## 6. Hiring and Firing

The **hiring/firing metaphor** is the most accessible and vivid expression of JTBD theory. It was popularized by Christensen and Moesta and reframes the entire relationship between customers and products.

### The Core Metaphor

People **"hire"** products and services when jobs arise in their lives, just as a manager hires an employee to fill a role. If the product does the job well, the customer hires it again. If it does a poor job, the customer **"fires"** it and looks for a replacement.

This language is deliberately chosen because it implies:
- **Agency**: The customer is the boss, not the product
- **Purpose**: Every hire has a specific reason -- the job
- **Accountability**: Products that don't perform get fired
- **Replaceability**: Any product can be fired and replaced by something that does the job better

### The Milkshake Story (the canonical example)

Christensen's team was hired by a fast-food chain (widely understood to be McDonald's) to improve milkshake sales. The company had already tried making them thicker, chocolatier, cheaper, and chunkier based on traditional customer research -- but nothing moved sales.

**The observation**: A researcher stood in a McDonald's for 18 hours taking notes. Nearly half of all milkshake sales occurred between 6:30 and 8:30 AM. The buyers were always alone, it was always the only item purchased, and they always drove away with it.

**The question**: Instead of "How can we improve the milkshake?" the team asked: "What job are you hiring the milkshake to do?"

**The morning job**: Commuters faced a long, boring drive. They needed something to:
- Keep them occupied during the drive (one hand on the wheel, the other holding something)
- Last the duration of the commute (the thick milkshake took ~23 minutes through a thin straw)
- Prevent hunger attacks at 10:00 AM
- Be easy to consume while driving (no mess, no crumbs)

**The real competition**: The milkshake wasn't competing against other milkshakes. It was competing against:
- **Bananas** -- finished too quickly (gone in 2 minutes)
- **Donuts** -- too sugary, left them feeling guilty, crumbs everywhere
- **Bagels with cream cheese** -- too messy to eat one-handed while driving
- **Snickers bars** -- too small, guilt-inducing, gone too fast

The milkshake won this competition because it was the best performer for the specific job.

**The afternoon job**: The same milkshake was hired for a completely different job in the afternoon. Parents were buying it for their kids. The job: be a way to say "yes" to a child and feel like a good parent. Here the competition was a trip to the toy store or an extra hour at the playground.

**The business impact**: Once the company understood the two jobs, it could improve the milkshake differently for each context. For the morning: make it thicker, add chunks of fruit for variety, move the machine to the front counter for faster service. For the afternoon: make it smaller and thinner (kids needed to finish it in a reasonable time). Milkshake sales reportedly increased sevenfold.

### "Firing" in Practice

Firing happens when:
- The product stops doing the job well (performance decline)
- A better alternative emerges that does the job more effectively (competitive displacement)
- The customer's circumstances change and the job itself changes (context shift)
- The customer discovers the product never did the job well -- they were just tolerating it (awareness)

Understanding firing is as valuable as understanding hiring. Moesta recommends interviewing people who recently fired your product with the same rigor you apply to those who hired it. "Firers reveal what your product fails to deliver; hirers reveal what it delivers well."

## 7. Non-Consumption as Competition

One of JTBD's most counterintuitive and powerful insights is that **the biggest competitor is often not another product -- it is doing nothing at all**.

### What is Non-Consumption?

Christensen defined non-consumption as the state where **potential customers can't find any solution that satisfies their job and opt to do nothing instead**. They either:
- Tolerate the status quo despite dissatisfaction
- Cobble together workarounds from disparate tools and behaviors
- Simply go without and accept the unresolved struggle

### The Competitive Landscape in JTBD

Instead of asking "Who else sells what we sell?" JTBD asks "What is the customer trying to accomplish -- and what other ways could they achieve it?"

This reveals a much broader set of "competitors":

1. **Direct competitors**: Other products in the same category (what traditional competitive analysis focuses on)
2. **Indirect competitors**: Products in different categories that address the same job (milkshakes vs. bananas)
3. **Workarounds**: Cobbled-together solutions using tools not designed for the job (spreadsheets, manual processes, asking a friend)
4. **DIY solutions**: Customers building their own solutions (homemade, self-taught, etc.)
5. **Non-consumption**: Doing nothing, tolerating the struggle, going without

### Why Non-Consumption Matters

Christensen argued that non-consumption is often **the most fertile ground for breakthrough innovation**. Companies fixate on stealing share from direct competitors, but the pool of non-consumers can be far larger than the existing market.

Examples:
- Before smartphones, the "non-consumers" of portable computing vastly outnumbered laptop users
- Before YouTube, the "non-consumers" of video education (people who wanted to learn but wouldn't enroll in a course) were enormous
- Before Duolingo, the "non-consumers" of language learning (people who wanted to learn but found courses too expensive, time-consuming, or intimidating) dwarfed the market for Rosetta Stone

### The Status Quo as the Strongest Competitor

Even when customers are dissatisfied, the status quo has powerful advantages:
- It is familiar (no learning curve)
- It is already integrated into their routines (habit)
- It carries no switching risk (no anxiety)
- It has zero additional cost (already paid for, in time or money)

Moesta's Four Forces framework explains this directly: the **Habit of the Present** and **Anxiety of the New** often outweigh the **Push** and **Pull** that would drive a switch. The result is that the customer stays in the status quo -- not because it's good, but because switching feels worse.

### Compensating Behaviors

A particularly valuable signal in JTBD research is **compensating behaviors** -- the creative workarounds customers invent when no good solution exists. These behaviors are:
- Evidence that a job exists and is important enough to motivate effort
- Indicators of willingness to pay (if they're already spending time/energy, they'll pay for something better)
- Design inspiration (the workaround reveals exactly how the customer thinks about the job)

Examples:
- Someone using a spreadsheet to track habits (compensating for no good habit-tracking app at the time)
- Someone listening to YouTube videos with screen off (compensating for no podcast version)
- Someone taking screenshots of recipes and saving them in a photos folder (compensating for no good recipe-saving tool)

### Implications for Innovators

1. **Don't just benchmark against competitors**: Benchmark against the status quo and non-consumption
2. **Look for workarounds**: They reveal the most urgent unmet jobs
3. **Reduce friction to near zero**: The bar for beating non-consumption is low on features but high on ease -- the solution must be dramatically easier than the status quo
4. **Quantify the non-consumption pool**: It is often 5-10x the size of the existing market

## 8. Applying JTBD to Content/Media

JTBD is directly applicable to content creation, YouTube channels, podcasts, and media products. A piece of content is a product that viewers/listeners/readers "hire" to do a job.

### The Core Question for Content Creators

**"What job is my audience hiring my content to do?"**

This is NOT the same as "What topic am I covering?" The topic is the supply-side view. The job is the demand-side view. Multiple creators can cover the same topic, but the viewer hires the one that best does their specific job.

### Common Jobs Content is Hired For

**Functional jobs:**
- Learn how to do something specific ("teach me to build a budget in Google Sheets")
- Stay informed about a topic area ("keep me current on AI developments")
- Make a decision ("help me choose between MacBook Air and Pro")
- Solve a problem right now ("fix my Python error")

**Emotional jobs:**
- Feel entertained during downtime ("make my commute enjoyable")
- Feel smart and informed ("make me feel like I understand this complex topic")
- Feel inspired and motivated ("make me believe I can do this too")
- Feel less alone in a struggle ("show me that others face this problem too")
- Feel calm or relaxed ("give me ambient background while I work")
- Avoid feeling bored ("fill dead time with something interesting")

**Social jobs:**
- Have something interesting to talk about ("give me conversation currency")
- Signal identity and values ("I'm the kind of person who watches this")
- Belong to a community ("make me feel part of the in-group")
- Appear knowledgeable to peers ("let me share this insight at work")

### The JTBD Content Formula

The Content Marketing Institute's JTBD formula for content:

> **"When I [context/situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [desired outcome]."**

Examples:
- "When I'm preparing for a job interview, I want to learn common behavioral questions, so I can feel confident and prepared."
- "When I'm on my morning run, I want to listen to something intellectually stimulating, so I can feel like I'm using my time productively."
- "When I don't understand a news event, I want a concise explainer, so I can participate in conversations without looking uninformed."

### Media Company Examples

**Netflix**: Users hire Netflix for multiple jobs:
- "Help me unwind after a stressful day" (relaxation)
- "Give me something my partner and I can enjoy together" (bonding)
- "Give me a show everyone is talking about" (social currency)
- Netflix famously said their biggest competitor is sleep -- because the real job is often "help me transition from being awake to being asleep" or "give me one more reason to stay up"

**Spotify**: Users hire Spotify to:
- "Create a desired vibe for my current activity" (working out, cooking, studying)
- "Discover music that matches my taste without effort" (curation)
- "Have background audio that improves my focus" (productivity companion)

**YouTube**: YouTube gets hired for a vast range of jobs, but the dominant ones include:
- "Teach me how to do X" (practical education -- the largest job category)
- "Entertain me during dead time" (commutes, waiting rooms, meals)
- "Help me feel connected to a creator/community I care about" (parasocial relationship, belonging)
- "Help me make a purchase decision" (reviews, comparisons)

### Non-Consumption in Content

For content creators, non-consumption includes:
- People who want to learn about a topic but haven't found a creator who explains it in a way they connect with
- People who want entertainment during commutes but listen to the radio because they haven't found the right podcast
- People who want to feel inspired but doom-scroll social media instead because it's lower effort

### Applying the Four Forces to Content Switching

Why does someone subscribe to a new YouTube channel (switch)?

- **Push**: Their current content sources are getting stale, repetitive, or no longer relevant. "I used to love that channel but it's been the same format for 2 years."
- **Pull**: The new channel has a distinctive voice, better production, or covers the topic in a way that resonates. "This creator explains things in a way I actually understand."
- **Anxiety**: "Will this channel actually stay good? Am I going to have to filter through a lot of bad content to find the gems? Is subscribing going to clutter my feed?"
- **Habit**: "But I already have my routine. I watch X every morning. My YouTube feed is already set up the way I like it."

### Practical Applications for a YouTube Channel

1. **Identify the job, not just the topic**: Don't say "I make videos about personal finance." Say "I help people in their 20s feel confident about money decisions they've been avoiding, so they can stop feeling anxious about their financial future."

2. **Audit against all three dimensions**: Is your content functionally useful (does it teach/inform)? Emotionally resonant (does it make them feel something)? Socially valuable (would they share it or reference it in conversation)?

3. **Study your "firers"**: Why do people unsubscribe? The reasons reveal where your content fails the job. Common reasons: quality declined, posting frequency changed, the creator's focus shifted away from what the viewer originally hired them for.

4. **Identify compensating behaviors**: What are your potential viewers currently doing instead of watching your content? Are they reading blog posts? Asking in Reddit forums? Watching competitors? Doing nothing? Each workaround is a clue about what the ideal content would look like.

5. **Reduce anxiety, break habits**: Make thumbnails and titles clearly signal what job the video will do. Use the first 10 seconds to confirm "this video is for you if..." Provide consistent formats so viewers know exactly what they're getting. All of these reduce the anxiety of clicking and break through the habit of skipping.

6. **Find non-consumption opportunities**: Look for topics where people desperately want to learn but existing content is too technical, too long, too boring, or too inaccessible. These are the milkshake-morning-commute opportunities -- massive unserved demand hiding behind non-consumption.

## Summary: Key Takeaways

| Concept | Core Insight |
|---------|-------------|
| **JTBD Theory** | People don't buy products; they hire them to make progress in their lives |
| **Four Forces** | Switching requires Push + Pull > Anxiety + Habit |
| **Three Job Types** | Every job has functional, emotional, and social dimensions |
| **ODI** | Use the Opportunity Algorithm (Importance + max(Importance - Satisfaction, 0)) to find underserved needs |
| **Switch Interviews** | Reconstruct the 6-stage timeline from First Thought to Ongoing Use |
| **Hiring/Firing** | Products are hired for jobs and fired when they stop performing |
| **Non-Consumption** | The biggest competitor is often "doing nothing" or cobbled workarounds |
| **Content Application** | Ask "What job is my content hired for?" across functional, emotional, and social dimensions |
